## It’s answers that matter

Asking good questions saves a lot of time, and I often tell my friends/coworkers to ask me as many questions as I ask them. I view it as a short-term investment with exponential returns in terms of clarity and context.

Like this post suggests: Good questions should be thoughtful, precise, and open-ended. But if your perfectly crafted 10/10 gourmet questions get redundant answers like below:

> Disclaimer: The examples portrayed below are slightly exaggerated for demonstration purposes; the odds of you receiving such garbage answers are lower nowadays.

**Question-1**: “I observed that you often read and talk about your work at X/LinkedIn. How did you approach that and what kind of advice would you give to anyone who’s also doing it?”

**Answer-1**: “Just do it. And keep doing it.”

Or something like:

**Question-2**: “How did you choose which book to read for ML? Would you recommend I read books as well?”

**Answer-2**: “Yes. Do it.”

Or something like:

**Question-3:** “I observed that you’re résumé has a lot of relevant MLE experience at multiple startups. Can you talk a little bit about how you got through?”

**Answer-3**: **“**I just asked them.**”**

It frustrates me, not because the answers are wrong, but because they’re right yet garbage and incredibly vague. It’s right that you should share your work on social media, you should read books and YES, you should send cold emails to people.

You can argue that in answers to questions like the social media/recruitment, there’s not much to be said, and that the person asking the question will reap exponential benefits out of simple actions, such as sharing their work or sending cold emails.

But tell me, my dear reader, would you not like answers like these:

**Answer-1** (Rephrased): I was observing that only a very small number of people were being creative about posting their daily logs. Everyone was just attaching screenshots and bullet points but not taking the time to actually talk about the work they’ve done that day. That’s where I differentiated myself. Social media is about selling your value addition and showing your audience that it’s worth it to spend 1–5 minutes of your time in my little corner of the internet. My advice to you: sit down and try thinking about how you can use your skills or learn new skills to show something that your audience will like. Remember, everything has an audience; you just have to think about which one’s right for you :)

**Answer-2** (Rephrased)**:** Good question, thanks for asking that! Firstly, I look for a few things when looking at books: how many pages does it have? Is the table of contents relevant to what I’m looking for? And is the content math-heavy or more beginner-friendly? Will this book fit within my time budget? Small questions like these help me prioritize what kind of books I like and what I don’t. And secondly, of course! I’d recommend everyone to sit and read books (if possible, in their full glory by ordering physical copies). and spend time pondering what they offer!

**Answer-3** (Rephrased): I noticed that they were hiring and have recently raised a lot of capital. So I tried my luck and reached out to them via email and LinkedIn. After a couple of interview rounds and negotiating, I got through! What I’d recommend you to regularly follow founders and try asking them, 99% of the time they’ll be willing to give you a shot if they like your work.

## Don’t give answers

Believe me, it doesn’t take a lot of time to rephrase your answers to be more engaging and informative like the ones above!

> **Here’s my hot take (close your eyes if you don’t like valid takes)**: we shouldn’t give direct answers at all. Rather, we should try talking through our answer, slopGPT conversation style (PLS FFS NO EMOJIS).

Direct answers often come with a lot of assumptions, the biggest one: **It worked for me; it may or may not work for you. (The classic).** It’s annoying, but it’s the truth. What worked for someone else may not work for you, but that doesn’t mean you should let someone get away with vague and uninformative answers.

Here’s what I do when trying to answer someone’s questions:

1. **Questions, more specifically counter questions** to narrow the scope of the answer down. It helps me be more informed and understand the person I’m trying to help.
2. **Be happy and energetic** when answering. I show my excitement because someone has taken the time to ask me questions!
3. **Try addressing naunces** when answering, **make jokes,** or pick specific points and try exploring some tangents. This often backfires because I have the memory of a goldfish, but I don’t care, I have fun doing it, and I try giving value to the person that’s on the receiving end.
4. **Give long answers;** oftentimes with a similar core message, but I put more effort into packaging that message to be more engaging and exciting.
5. I try to be **patient and polite,** and don’t make many assumptions about the person’s intellectual levels or experience. Every bit of context is gained via constant questioning.

But why does this make my answers any better? Good question, and to be honest, it doesn’t always make your answers better. If the person asking is in a rush/a deadline or if it’s a coworker, I or you’d have to resort to shorter but precise answers. I only follow these pointers if the person assures me that they have the time to listen.

In those cases, I think that the above pointers back my hot take; to treat the person on the other end as a person, and not as an interviewer. Trying your best to ensure that the person on the other end feels cared for, and giving time and attention to the minor details, is a great way to make friends and also improve your perspective (sometimes).

Asking good questions saves us a lot of time, but it’s only half the story. If the answers you receive are not on par with the question’s quality, you have to dig deeper and ask more questions.

So, my dear readers, I hope you and I can spend more time giving more elaborate and informative answers. Don’t treat people asking questions as interviewers, but people that you can have conversations with.

Thank you for your time!
